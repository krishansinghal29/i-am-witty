## 1. Who the coach is

**Patrick King** is a San Francisco–based **"social interaction specialist" / conversation
coach** and one of the most prolific self-published authors in the social-skills space. He
writes under the **Social Skills Coaching** brand and runs **Patrick King Consulting**
(patrickkingconsulting.com). He has authored **40+ books** on conversation, charisma,
likability, banter, and social skills — the most relevant to this project being a tight
cluster on **quick wit and conversation**:

- **_The Art of Witty Banter: Be Clever, Be Quick, Be Interesting_** (2016/2020) — his
  flagship quick-wit title.
- **_Improve Your Conversations: Think on Your Feet, Witty Banter, and Always Know What to
  Say with Improv Comedy Techniques_** (2015, 2nd ed. 2020).
- **_Better Small Talk: Talk to Anyone, Avoid Awkwardness, Generate Deep Conversations, and
  Make Real Friends_** (2020).
- **_Conversation Tactics: Strategies to Charm, Befriend, and Defend_** (and the
  Wittiness/Banter/Likability volume).
- Adjacent humor titles: **_Laugh Tactics_** and **_How to Be Hilarious and Quick-Witted in
  Everyday Conversation_**; the catch-all **_How to Talk to Anyone_**.

He also hosts the **Social Skills Coaching** podcast (each episode typically unpacks one
named technique from the books, e.g. "Helpful Acronyms," "The Witty Comeback Machine,"
"Ironic Humor"), and his consulting site offers **1:1 coaching** plus free blog articles and
at least one first-party lead-magnet worksheet.

**Why he matters for this project:** King is the **single highest-value quick-wit source we
have**. Unlike most charisma coaches, he reduces "being quick and witty" to a set of
**named, memorizable frameworks and rehearsable solo drills** — the conversational analogue
to Greg Dean's joke mechanics. His core acronyms (**HPM / SBR / EDR**), his **improv-comedy
rules ported to small talk**, his **witty-comeback formula**, and his **free-association
warm-up drills** map almost one-to-one onto runnable app exercises, many of which can be
practiced **alone, off-line, before you ever talk to a person**. That solo-practice angle is
a near-perfect fit for an anxious-introvert audience.

**Audience:** socially anxious / introverted adults, "I never know what to say" / "I go
blank" people, daters, professionals who want to be more likable — explicitly the
think-on-your-feet, fear-of-awkward-silence crowd.

## 3. The methodology (theory of improvement)

King's "theory of wit" rests on a few load-bearing claims, several borrowed explicitly from
**improv comedy**:

1. **Wit is a system, not a gift.** "Quick" people aren't smarter; they run **pre-installed
   frameworks** so they never face a blank slate. Memorize a small number of acronyms/moves
   and you always have *somewhere to go*. This is the spine of the whole catalog.

2. **You always have three directions to go.** For any statement, you can respond about
   yourself (**HPM** — History / Philosophy / Metaphor), ask outward about the topic
   (**SBR** — Specific / Broad / Related), or turn toward the *person* (**EDR** — Emotion /
   Detail / Restatement). Knowing these means you literally cannot run out of responses.

3. **Conversation is improv; run the improv rules.** From _Improve Your Conversations_:
   **"Yes, AND…"** (accept and build, never block), **react to everything** (it was put
   there for a reason), **be specific** (specific details give people something to grab),
   **no "No"** (never lead with negation), **create motion** (keep the ball moving), and
   **it's entertainment, not an interview** (stop interrogating). Treat the other person as a
   **scene partner / teammate**, not an opponent.

4. **Stop being literal and reactive — be playful.** Wit comes from *choosing* a non-obvious
   response: misinterpret things on purpose, exaggerate, break the fourth wall, riff. Most
   people answer literally and kill the energy; the witty person picks the funnier read.

5. **Pre-load your material.** You don't improvise from zero — you **prepare** a
   "conversation résumé" and a stock of **mini-stories / fallback stories** so that in the
   moment you're *retrieving*, not *inventing*. Spontaneity is rehearsed.

6. **Slow down to seem quick.** The **two-second rule** (pause ~2s before responding) makes
   you seem more thoughtful and more attentive, not slower — counterintuitive but central.

7. **Tell tiny stories, not status updates.** Replace one-word answers ("Good," "Fine")
   with a **1:1:1 mini-story** (one event, one sentence, one emotion), starting as close to
   the end as possible. Stories beat facts for connection.

8. **Humor is mechanical too.** Comebacks = **agree + amplify to the absurd** (or
   **deliberately misinterpret** the literal words in your favor). Plus comic tools:
   **the comic triple**, **sarcasm**, **irony**, **hyperbole**, **callbacks/inside jokes**,
   and **good-natured teasing** (only on things the person doesn't tie to their identity).

In short: **install the acronyms so you're never blank → run improv rules so you build instead
of block → choose the playful/non-literal response → and pre-load résumé + mini-stories so
"spontaneous" is actually prepared.**

## 4. Reconstructed curriculum (the codified system, mapped to drills)

### A. The "always have something to say" acronyms (core of Witty Banter & Better Small Talk)
- **HPM (History / Philosophy / Metaphor)** — respond *about yourself*: a personal
  experience ("That reminds me of the last time I skied…"), an opinion/stance ("I've always
  loved skiing because…"), or a comparison/metaphor ("Skiing is basically falling with
  style").
- **SBR (Specific / Broad / Related)** — ask *outward about the topic*: specific ("Which
  slopes?"), broad ("Where else have you skied?"), or related/tangential ("Ever tried
  snowboarding?" / "I love weekend trips").
- **EDR (Emotion / Detail / Restatement)** — turn *toward the person*: name their emotion
  ("That must have been amazing!"), ask for detail with the 5 Ws ("When did you start?"), or
  restate to show listening ("So you went up to the mountains last weekend?"). Best for
  people in difficulty / emotional moments.
- **Conversational threading** — pull any "thread" out of what someone said and follow it.
  "I went skiing with my brother last weekend" contains ≥3 threads (skiing / last weekend /
  brother); comment or ask about any one. The meta-skill the acronyms feed.
- **FORD (Family / Occupation / Recreation / Dreams)** — four reliable small-talk topic
  buckets to generate questions from when you're stuck (a classic he teaches in the small-talk
  material).

### B. The improv-comedy rules (Improve Your Conversations — the chapter set)
- **"Yes, AND…"** — accept the other person's reality and *add* to it; never block.
- **Read and react to everything** — react to whatever's offered ("it was put there for a
  reason"); make the reaction **bigger and slower** than feels natural.
- **Be specific (not generic)** — give specific, concrete details others can grab and run
  with; replace vague answers with vivid ones.
- **Details, details, details** — layer concrete sensory detail to make answers come alive.
- **No "No"** — never lead with negation/contradiction; it kills the shared direction.
- **Create motion** — keep observing/adapting so the conversation flows and never stalls.
- **It's entertainment, not an interview** — stop interrogating; aim to entertain/connect.
- **Think on your feet / support your teammates** — treat your partner as a scene partner
  you're making look good, not an opponent.

### C. The quick-wit / "never go blank" drills (Witty Banter)
- **Never speak in absolutes** — don't ask all-or-nothing questions ("What's your #1
  favorite movie?"); ask option-rich ones ("What are some movies you love?") so people can
  answer easily.
- **Think before you react / the Two-Second Rule** — pause ~2 seconds before responding;
  find the primary emotion and express it bigger and slower; seems more attentive and witty.
- **Practice free association** — solo warm-up drills (see §E) to train fast, fluid
  idea-linking so responses come instantly.
- **Break the fourth wall** — step outside the conversation and comment on it ("Wow, this
  went in a weird direction, didn't it?"; "Did we really just bond over a '90s boy band?";
  naming an awkward silence). Defuses tension and signals composure.
- **"Us against the world" technique** — bond by jointly reacting to your shared environment
  ("It's so loud in here, right?"), creating instant team-feeling.
- **Derail your train of thought** — deliberately abandon the obvious/literal line and take a
  surprising tangent to be more interesting.

### D. The humor / comeback toolkit (Witty Banter + Laugh Tactics + How to Be Hilarious)
- **The Witty Comeback Machine — agree & amplify** — receive an insult, *agree* with it, then
  *exaggerate it to an absurd degree*. ("Terrible cook?" → "Yeah, my eggs double as enemy
  poison.") Projects confidence; the insult rolls off.
- **Go beyond the literal / favorable misinterpretation** — deliberately misread the literal
  words in your favor. ("Slow as a glacier!" → "You mean strong and cool under pressure?")
- **The comic triple** — list two straight items then a third absurd one for the laugh
  ("I packed sunscreen, snacks, and three existential crises").
- **Sarcasm / irony / hyperbole** — state the opposite of what's true (with tone), juxtapose
  opposites, or exaggerate to absurd extremes for comic effect.
- **Good-natured teasing** — tease only on things the person *doesn't* tie to their identity;
  delivery (warmth/smile) makes or breaks it.
- **Callbacks / inside jokes** — reintroduce an earlier funny moment later to create shared
  history and "you had to be there" intimacy.
- **Double explanations** — give a short witty answer first, then a second, more genuine
  explanation, so you're playful *and* substantive.

### E. Solo practice drills (mostly from Witty Banter / Better Small Talk — the gold for us)
- **Five-Word Free Association** — write 5 random words; rapidly generate 3 associations for
  each; chain the last word into a new round; repeat 3×. Trains fluid, blank-proof thinking.
- **Rapid Verbal Association** — pick a random dictionary word and *say aloud* 15 associations
  as fast as possible; repeat. Builds speed.
- **Dictionary Story Creation** — pick 2 random words, treat them as a company name, and
  instantly improvise a short story; speed over polish.
- **Five-Word Story Weaving** — pick 5 random words and weave them into one coherent story on
  the spot; speed over quality.
- **Read-Aloud Emotional Warm-up** — read an emotionally rich passage aloud, exaggerating
  every emotion (scream/whisper/laugh), varying pace and inflection, breathing from the
  diaphragm; compare your 3rd read to your 1st. Trains vocal expressiveness/reactivity.

### F. Storytelling-in-conversation drills (Better Small Talk)
- **1:1:1 Mini-Story** — turn a flat answer into a story with **one event, one sentence
  setup, one emotion**, starting as close to the end as possible ("Good" → "I almost got
  taken out by a hyperactive squirrel on my hike this morning").
- **The Story Spine** — the expanded structure: *Once upon a time… Every day… But one day…
  Because of that (×2–3, escalating)… Until finally… And ever since then…* Use to shape a
  longer conversational story.
- **Pinning the Tail** — after someone's story, add your perspective and *amplify their
  primary emotion* rather than topping it.

### G. Preparation drills (Better Small Talk / Witty Banter)
- **Conversation Résumé** — pre-write 4 buckets of talking points: (1) this week / daily life,
  (2) broader life & background/hobbies, (3) notable experiences, accomplishments, strengths,
  travel, (4) current-events takes & funny recent situations. Pull from it under pressure.
- **Fallback Stories** — prepare a handful of go-to anecdotes, each with a bridge sentence + an
  opinion + a follow-up question, so you always have a deployable story ready.
- **Mini-Story Stockpile** — pre-craft 3-sentence anecdotes for the predictable questions
  (work, hometown, weekend, news) so common prompts never catch you flat.

### H. Eliciting / icebreaking (Better Small Talk)
- **Three indirect openers** — ask for info/opinion ("Do you know when this starts?"), comment
  on the shared context ("The lighting here is gorgeous"), or find commonality ("So how do you
  know the host?").
- **Elicitation moves** — recognition, specific compliments, shared complaining, mild
  correction, and **naïveté** (assume/guess their situation to prompt them to teach you).
- **"Third things"** — ask about an external topic (news, nature, the venue) to learn
  someone's personality indirectly without putting them on the spot.
