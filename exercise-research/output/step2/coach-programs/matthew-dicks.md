## 1. Who the coach is

**Matthew Dicks** is an American novelist, elementary-school teacher, and one of the
most decorated live storytellers in the US: a **60+-time Moth StorySLAM champion**
and **9-time Moth GrandSLAM champion**. He is the author of **_Storyworthy: Engage,
Teach, Persuade, and Change Your Life through the Power of Storytelling_** (New World
Library, 2018; foreword/collab with Dan Kennedy of The Moth) and the follow-up
**_Someday Is Today_**. With his wife **Elysha Dicks** he founded **Speak Up**, a
Hartford-based storytelling organization, and co-hosts the **Speak Up Storytelling**
podcast, where they dissect a real told story each episode against his toolkit.

He sells his curriculum two ways: **live/corporate workshops** (he teaches
storytelling and public speaking to companies, universities, religious institutions,
school districts and conferences worldwide) and a growing **online course catalog +
membership** at **storyworthymd.com** ("Storyworthy MD" / the Storyworthy Academy and
Community).

**Why he matters for this project:** Dicks has the most *codified, named-device*
system for **personal/true storytelling** of any coach we've surveyed — the
storytelling analogue to Greg Dean's joke mechanics. His central insight is that
ordinary people already live story-worthy lives and just lack (a) a method to *find*
those stories and (b) a vocabulary of *devices* to tell them well. Almost every
concept maps cleanly to a solo, repeatable drill — a daily journaling habit, a
freewriting timer, a conversation game, or a craft constraint applied to a draft —
which makes him a goldmine for runnable exercises. He is also explicitly
**non-comedian / non-performer friendly** ("you don't need to be funny to be
funny"), which fits an anxious-introvert audience well.

**Audience:** complete beginners who "don't have any stories" through working
storytellers and public speakers; plus a heavy **business / professional** track
(founders, marketers, teachers, leaders) who want to be more memorable than data
alone.

## 3. The methodology (theory of improvement)

Dicks' system rests on a small set of load-bearing claims:

1. **Every great story is about a Five-Second Moment.** Regardless of length, a story
   is really about *one five-second moment of transformation* — the instant you fall
   in/out of love, change your mind, forgive, despair, or realize something. You must
   show you "walked in as one person and walked out as another." Everything else in
   the story exists only to set up that moment. This is the system's north star: find
   the five-second moment first, build the story around it.

2. **You already have thousands of stories — you just can't see them yet.** The
   bottleneck is *attention*, not *experience*. A daily noticing practice (**Homework
   for Life**) installs a "storytelling lens" that makes everyday moments visible and,
   he claims, "slows down time." Finding stories is a trainable habit, not a gift.

3. **Tell it like you'd tell a friend at dinner (the Dinner Test).** Authentic,
   first-person, vulnerable, conversational — no purple prose, no invented dialogue,
   no theatrical hand-waving. If you wouldn't say it that way at dinner, cut it.

4. **Stories run on causation, not chronology (But-and-Therefore).** Scenes connected
   by "and then" have no momentum; scenes connected (explicitly or implicitly) by
   **but / therefore / so / because / instead / as a result** create change and
   forward motion. (He borrows the South Park "but/therefore vs. and-then" rule.)

5. **Build a movie in the audience's mind.** Every moment needs a *location*; every
   moment is a *scene with a setting*. Provide visual anchors and the film runs in
   their heads. Avoid thesis statements / exposition — give the evidence first and the
   meaning last (the reverse of the five-paragraph essay), which is what lets you
   surprise.

6. **Start at the opposite, end on the heart.** Find the *opposite* of your
   transformation and start there ("I once was X … now I am Y"); start as close to the
   end as possible; end *on the heart* (emotion/meaning), never on a laugh. Make the
   stakes **small and relatable** — find "the little moments hidden inside big
   moments."

7. **Engineer stakes, suspense, surprise, and humor with named devices.** Rather than
   "make it exciting," he gives concrete tools: the **Elephant**, **Backpack**,
   **Breadcrumbs**, **Hourglass**, **Crystal Ball** for stakes/suspense; **Babies &
   Blenders** and **Milk Cans & a Baseball** for humor; **Five Permissible Lies** for
   shaping the truth. The only way to make someone cry is **surprise**, achieved by
   hiding information (bombs in clutter, distance, camouflage with laughter) and
   heightening **contrast**.

In short: **find the five-second moment → tell it like dinner → connect with
but/therefore → run a movie in their minds → engineer stakes/surprise/humor with
named devices.** Habits to find stories, constraints to tell them.

## 4. Reconstructed curriculum (the codified system, mapped to drills)

### A. Finding stories (the "Storyworthy Moments" track)
- **Homework for Life** — nightly, ask "What is the most story-worthy moment of my
  day? / What made today different from any other day?"; write date + a one-sentence
  summary of a five-second moment in a spreadsheet. Few sentences max; never miss a
  day. Builds a treasury of hundreds of story seeds and a storytelling lens.
- **Crash & Burn** — 10–15 min timed stream-of-consciousness freewriting; 3 rules:
  don't attach to any idea, don't judge any thought, never let the pen stop (fall back
  to listing colors/numbers/countries when stuck). Mine the output afterward for story
  seeds.
- **First, Last, Best, Worst** — table of prompts (kiss, car, job, pet, trouble…);
  for each, recall your first/last/best/worst instance in a few words; surfaces
  forgotten memories; also a conversation/first-date/car-ride game.
- **Three-Two-One** — a game-based method to turn random memories into structured
  story ideas (workshop drill).
- **Where Oh Where** — use physical locations/places as emotional triggers to surface
  stories tied to a setting.
- **Lost & Found** — mine universal stories from moments of loss and rediscovery.

### B. Structuring the story
- **Find the Five-Second Moment** (the transformation) — write it first.
- **Start at the opposite / end on the heart** — begin in the opposite state, end on
  the emotional moment of change; start near the end.
- **But-and-Therefore pass** — rewrite "and then" connections into but/therefore
  causation.
- **Movie-in-the-mind / set every scene** — give every moment a location.
- **The Dinner Test** — sanity-check tone/authenticity.

### C. Stakes & suspense (Dicks' five stakes/suspense devices)
- **The Elephant** — state the need/want/problem/peril/mystery in the first ~30
  seconds so the audience knows a story is starting and has a reason to wonder.
- **The Backpack** — load the audience with your hopes/fears *before* an event so they
  share your anticipation (best when the plan fails).
- **Breadcrumbs** — hint at a future event, revealing just enough to keep them
  guessing (great when the truly unexpected is coming).
- **The Hourglass** — when they're hanging on every word near the payoff, *slow down*
  / stop the story; describe things that don't need describing to stretch the wait.
- **The Crystal Ball** — make a plausible false prediction about what's about to
  happen, so the audience wonders whether it'll come true.

### D. Surprise (how to make someone cry)
- **Avoid thesis statements** (evidence first, meaning last).
- **Heighten contrast** between the surprise and the moment before it.
- **Plant bombs** — hide a key detail in clutter, place it far from the payoff, or
  camouflage it with laughter so the reveal lands harder.

### E. Humor (the "Storytelling with Humor" track)
- **Babies & Blenders** — jam two things that rarely go together to create comic
  incongruity.
- **Milk Cans & a Baseball** — setup/punchline structure: stack "milk cans" (setup
  language that builds anticipation) and save the funniest word/idea for last (the
  "baseball"); more cans = bigger potential laugh.
- **Comic definition** — define an object/person/idea in its most basic essence,
  choosing only the surprising/amusing/unspoken aspects.
- **Laugh-laugh-laugh-cry** — get a laugh in the first 30 seconds; make them laugh
  before they cry ("it hurts more that way"); humor camouflages and resets tension.

### F. The Five Permissible Lies (shaping the truth without fabricating)
**Omission**, **Compression** (years are boring, days are thrilling), **Assumption**
(reasonable guesses about forgotten detail), **Progression** (reorder events for
emotional payoff), **Conflation** (compress a long change into one moment) — only ever
for the audience's benefit, never to invent what didn't happen.

### G. Performance / delivery rules
Don't memorize word-for-word (memorize only first lines, last lines, and the scenes);
no rhetorical questions; no props; never address the audience or say the word "story";
use a mic; find 3 smiling/nodding faces (left/center/right) as guideposts; manage —
don't perform — your emotions; a little nervousness is endearing. (Drilled live in
workshops via *Practice Your Stories* and group feedback.)
